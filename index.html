The leadership of the Ohio General Assembly guides the legislative agenda for each chamber. The Speaker of the House and President of the Senate are the highest-ranking officers, presiding over their respective chambers and appointing committee chairs. The Majority Leader coordinates the majority party's legislative strategy, while the Minority Leader leads the opposition. Whips help ensure party members are present and voting as a bloc. 📖 About the Committee System

Committee Assignments

The Senate President names the standing committees of the Senate and appoints their members, ranking minority members, and chairpersons at the beginning of session. The Minority Leader may recommend minority party members for each committee.

The chairpersons of House committees are appointed by the Speaker, who also determines the number of members on each committee and the ratio of majority to minority party members.

Role of the Chairperson

A chairperson and vice-chairperson are named from the majority party members. The chairperson exercises a great deal of authority — determining when the committee meets, which bills will receive a public hearing, and the duration of testimony.

Standing Committees

The most active type of committee is the standing committee. Each house has separate standing committees to deal with broad subject matter areas. The number and titles may vary from General Assembly to General Assembly.

Rules and Reference Committees

The Rules and Reference Committees assign bills and joint resolutions to the appropriate standing committees and may recall any bill from a committee at any time. Their actions are critical to the progress of any legislation toward enactment.

📍 Ohio Redistricting

How Districts Work

All information on this page was collected from sources published by the Ohio Legislative Service Commission.

Drawing General Assembly Districts

The Ohio Redistricting Commission

Under the Ohio Constitution, the seven-member Ohio Redistricting Commission divides Ohio into Senate and House districts. Its members are the Governor, Auditor of State, Secretary of State, and four persons chosen by legislative leadership. No appointed member may be a current member of Congress.

The Commission meets in years ending in "1" and must adopt a redistricting plan by September 15 by a specified bipartisan vote. The plan generally cannot be changed until the next decennial redistricting.

redistricting.ohio.gov ↗

District Standards

Ohio's population is divided by 99 (House ratio) and by 33 (Senate ratio) to set equal representation targets. Each district must contain between 95%–105% of the applicable ratio.

The Commission must attempt to draw a plan that:

  • Is not drawn primarily to favor or disfavor a political party
  • Corresponds closely to statewide partisan preferences
  • Includes compact districts

🏛️ House Districts

Must be contiguous, bounded by a single nonintersecting line. Counties with population >105% of the ratio may have multiple wholly-contained districts.

⚖️ Senate Districts

Must be composed of three contiguous House districts. Counties with population equal to one or more Senate ratios get that many Senate districts within their boundaries.

Federal Congressional Districts

Congressional districts are also redrawn in years ending in "1." Based on the 2020 census, Ohio has 15 U.S. House seats with an ideal district population of 786,629.

The General Assembly must adopt a plan by September 30. If it fails, the Redistricting Commission has until October 31, then the General Assembly gets until November 30.

Key Redistricting Concepts

📐 Contiguity

Every district must be a single unbroken shape. Its boundary must be a single nonintersecting continuous line — preventing "donut" districts where one district entirely surrounds another.

📏 Compactness

A compact district minimizes distance between all parts of its territory. Compactness is measured by perimeter length or average distance from edges to center — shorter means more compact.

🏙️ Keeping Subdivisions Whole

Both redistricting processes prioritize keeping counties, cities, villages, and townships together. Splitting is only permitted when a subdivision's population exceeds the ideal district size.

If a city straddles a county line, drawing the district boundary along that county line is not considered splitting the city — even if the city ends up in two districts.

🔴🔵 Packing and Cracking

Packing concentrates a group into a supermajority in one district, limiting broader influence. Cracking splits a group across many districts where they form only a minority. Both practices can give rise to gerrymandering claims.

⚖️ Majority-Minority Districts

The Equal Protection Clause and the Voting Rights Act of 1965 prohibit plans that abridge citizens' right to vote on account of race or language minority status. Courts may require majority-minority districts to remedy vote dilution.

📜 Ohio Legislature

How a Bill Becomes Law

A step-by-step guide to Ohio's legislative process — from identifying a need for legislation to a law taking effect.

The Legislative Process
1
Introduction
The bill's title is read by the clerk on the day of introduction. In the Senate, a Senator files four copies with the Senate Clerk at least one hour before session. In the House, filed electronically. The bill is numbered and referred to the Rules and Reference Committee.
2
Committee Assignment
The Rules and Reference Committee reviews the bill and recommends it to the appropriate standing committee. This referral constitutes the bill's second consideration.
3
Standing Committee Review
The committee holds public hearings. It may amend the bill, create a substitute bill, refer it to a subcommittee, postpone it indefinitely (defeating it), or favorably report it back to the full chamber.
4
Third Consideration & Floor Vote
The Rules and Reference Committee schedules the bill for floor debate. Members may speak, offer amendments, and vote. A majority vote is required to pass. This constitutes the third consideration.
5
Passed Both Chambers
The bill must pass both the House and Senate in identical form. If one chamber amends the other's version, a conference committee reconciles the differences before final passage.
6
Governor's Action
The Act is presented to the Governor. If signed, it becomes law. If the Governor takes no action within 10 days (Sundays excluded), it becomes law automatically. If vetoed, it returns to the originating chamber for a potential override vote.
✓
Effective Date
After filing with the Secretary of State, the law takes effect on the 91st day — allowing time for a possible referendum petition. Emergency laws, tax levies, and appropriations for current state expenses take effect immediately.
Committee Actions

A committee may take several actions on a bill:

  • Report the bill favorably with no changes
  • Adopt amendments and report it as an amended bill
  • Redraft and report it as a substitute bill
  • Indefinitely postpone the bill (defeating it)
  • Take no action at all

A majority vote of committee members is required to favorably report or indefinitely postpone a bill. Members must be present to vote — proxy votes are not permitted.

Types of Bills & Resolutions

📋 Bills

A bill proposes to enact, amend, or repeal a law. After passing both chambers it becomes an "Act," then a "law" upon the Governor's signature or after 10 days without action.

🤝 Joint Resolutions

Used to ratify proposed amendments to the U.S. Constitution, call for a federal constitutional convention, or propose amendments to the Ohio Constitution. Require both chambers and filing with the Secretary of State.

📝 Concurrent Resolutions

Require both chambers but are not filed with the Secretary of State. Used to memorialize Congress, determine joint procedural matters, and commend people or events of interest to both chambers.

📄 Simple Resolutions

Require approval of only the originating chamber. Used for each chamber's internal procedural matters and expressions of opinion that don't require action from the other chamber.
